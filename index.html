SIMULATED PHISHING TEST

You just clicked a phishing link.

Good news: this one was a test. Bad news: in a real attack, that single click could have handed a criminal your login, your inbox, and a way into the rest of the business.

Nothing was compromised. No credentials were captured and nothing was installed. This was a controlled exercise run by Central Telecoms IT to help everyone spot the real thing. Take two minutes to read the rest of this page — that's the whole point.

What just happened

You received an email designed to look legitimate and clicked a link inside it. Real phishing emails are built to do exactly this — create a reason to click before you have time to think. Attackers only need it to work once.

Clicking is recorded against your account for this exercise so the team can measure how the business responds and where extra training helps. This is about building the habit, not catching anyone out.

The red flags to catch next time

  • !

    A sense of urgency

    "Act now", "your account will be suspended", "final notice". Pressure is the point — it stops you checking.

  • !

    The real sender address

    The display name can say anything. Check the actual address behind it, and be wary of look-alike domains (rn for m, .co for .com.au).

  • !

    Where the link really goes

    Hover over a link (or long-press on mobile) before tapping. If the destination doesn't match the sender, don't click.

  • !

    A request for your password or MFA code

    Legitimate services never ask you to confirm your password or one-time code via a link. Ever.

  • !

    Something that's slightly off

    Odd greetings, unexpected attachments, small spelling or formatting mistakes, or a request that just doesn't fit your normal workflow.

What to do from here

  1. Slow down. If an email pressures you to act fast, that's the moment to stop.
  2. Verify through a channel you trust — call the person, or open the site by typing the address yourself rather than clicking.
  3. Report anything suspicious. Reporting a real phishing email early can protect the whole company.
  4. Never enter your password or MFA code on a page you reached by clicking an email link.
